# Fiber channel physics

Every optical link in this book, from a two-kilometre data-center interconnect to a transpacific submarine cable, is a waveguide made of glass that does four things to the light it carries. It attenuates it. It delays different frequencies by different amounts. It couples the two polarization modes in a way that drifts with time and temperature. And, because the field is confined to a core whose area is of order a hundred square micrometres over tens of kilometres, it responds to the light's own intensity. Amplifiers then restore the power at the price of added noise, and the interplay between that noise and the intensity-dependent distortion sets a maximum signal-to-noise ratio that no transceiver can exceed.

This chapter builds the physics of that channel from first principles to the engineering rules that operators use. The plan is linear. Section 1.1 explains where loss comes from and which standardized fibers exist. Section 1.2 expands the propagation constant to second and third order and shows why intensity-modulated, direct-detection (IM/DD) links in data centers live near the zero-dispersion wavelength (零色散波长). Section 1.3 treats polarization as a random walk on the Poincaré sphere. Section 1.4 derives the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (非线性薛定谔方程). Section 1.5 derives amplifier noise and the "fifty-eight-decibel rule". Section 1.6 combines Sections 1.4 and 1.5 into the Gaussian-noise (GN) model and its optimum launch power. Section 1.7 surveys hollow-core and space-division-multiplexed fibers, which change some of the constants of the earlier sections.

Two conventions run through the chapter. First, every number in the prose is either taken from a recorded source (shown with a bracketed citation) or computed by a script in `book/calc/ch01/` from such sources; whenever a calculation needs a modeling choice that no source fixes, such as a span length or a launch power, we say "we assume" and list the choice in the chapter's assumption file. Second, we write the optical field as $E \propto A(z,t)\,e^{i(\beta_0 z-\omega_0 t)}$, so a positive $\beta_2$ means normal dispersion; communications papers that use $e^{+j\omega t}$ flip the sign of the $\beta_2$ term, and we flag the places where this matters.

## Attenuation and fiber types

### Why loss is the first constraint

Light launched into a fiber loses power at a rate proportional to the power present, $dP/dz = -\alpha P$, so that $P(L) = P(0)\,e^{-\alpha L}$. Engineers quote the coefficient in decibels per kilometre, $\alpha_{\mathrm{dB}} = 10\log_{10}(e)\,\alpha \approx 4.343\,\alpha$ with $\alpha$ in $\mathrm{km}^{-1}$. Through the amplifier gain needed to undo it, span loss sets the noise floor of every amplified system (Section 1.5). Attenuation (衰减) is therefore the first number one asks of any fiber.

### Decomposing the loss spectrum

Kanamori's half-century review writes the total loss of a silica fiber as a sum of physically distinct mechanisms {{S:kanamori_ieice_2021}}:
$$
\alpha_{\mathrm{dB}}(\lambda) = \frac{A_R}{\lambda^{4}} + B + \alpha_{\mathrm{imp}}(\lambda) + \alpha_{\mathrm{def}}(\lambda) + E_1 e^{E_2/\lambda} + \alpha_{\mathrm{IR},1} e^{-\alpha_{\mathrm{IR},2}/\lambda}.
$$
The first term is Rayleigh scattering (瑞利散射) with coefficient $A_R$; $B$ collects wavelength-independent imperfection losses (core–cladding interface roughness, micro-bending); $\alpha_{\mathrm{imp}}$ is impurity absorption, dominated by the OH⁻ ion; $\alpha_{\mathrm{def}}$ is absorption by glass defects; the $E$-term is the ultraviolet Urbach tail of electronic absorption and the last term is the infrared multiphonon tail. (We rename two of Kanamori's symbols: his $D(\lambda)$ for defect absorption would collide with the dispersion parameter $D$, and his $F_1,F_2$ with the noise factor $F$.)

Three of these terms are intrinsic to silica and cannot be engineered away; the others are extrinsic. The intrinsic ones produce the familiar "V" shape: Rayleigh scattering falls as $\lambda^{-4}$, infrared absorption rises steeply beyond about the C-band, and the minimum sits where they cross. For silica the infrared tail contributes {{L:ch01.phys.ir_absorption_1550}} at the C-band centre, and the ultraviolet Urbach tail is negligible at telecom wavelengths {{Q:ch01.phys.ir_absorption_1550}}. Among extrinsic terms, the first overtone of the OH vibration sits at {{L:ch01.phys.oh_overtone_1380}}, which is why ITU-T distinguishes the "low-water-peak" G.652.D category from G.652.B by an attenuation limit near that wavelength, {{L:ch01.att.g652d_max_1383_after_h2}} after hydrogen ageing.

### Deriving the λ⁻⁴ law from dipole radiation

Consider a glass that is homogeneous on average but whose relative permittivity fluctuates, $\varepsilon_r(\mathbf{r}) = \bar\varepsilon_r + \Delta\varepsilon_r(\mathbf{r})$, with correlation volume $\delta V$ much smaller than $\lambda^3$. A plane wave of amplitude $E_0$ and intensity $I = \tfrac12 c\varepsilon_0|E_0|^2$ polarizes each small volume beyond the average, inducing an excess dipole moment
$$
\mathbf{p} = \varepsilon_0\,\Delta\varepsilon_r\,\delta V\,\mathbf{E}_0 .
$$
An oscillating dipole radiates the time-averaged power $P_{\mathrm{rad}} = \omega^4|\mathbf{p}|^2/(12\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3)$. Substituting,
$$
P_{\mathrm{rad}} = \frac{\omega^4\varepsilon_0^2\,\Delta\varepsilon_r^2\,\delta V^2\,|E_0|^2}{12\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3} = \frac{k^4}{6\pi}\,\Delta\varepsilon_r^2\,\delta V^2\, I, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}.
$$
Because the fluctuations in different correlation volumes are uncorrelated, their scattered powers add incoherently. There are $1/\delta V$ volumes per unit volume, so the power removed per unit length per unit intensity, which is the scattering coefficient, is
$$
\alpha_R = \frac{1}{\delta V}\cdot\frac{k^4}{6\pi}\,\langle\Delta\varepsilon_r^2\rangle\,\delta V^2 = \frac{8\pi^3}{3\lambda^4}\,\langle\Delta\varepsilon_r^2\rangle\,\delta V .
$$
The $\lambda^{-4}$ dependence is now explicit: it is the $\omega^4$ of dipole radiation. What remains is the physics of $\langle\Delta\varepsilon_r^2\rangle\delta V$.

In a one-component glass the fluctuations are fluctuations of density $\rho$. Molten silica is in thermodynamic equilibrium, and the density fluctuations of a volume $\delta V$ obey the Einstein–Smoluchowski result $\langle\Delta\rho^2\rangle/\rho^2 = k_B T\,\kappa_T/\delta V$, with $\kappa_T$ the isothermal compressibility. On cooling, the structure stops relaxing at the fictive temperature $T_f$, and the fluctuations present at $T_f$ are frozen in. Writing $\Delta\varepsilon_r = \rho\,(\partial\varepsilon_r/\partial\rho)\,\Delta\rho/\rho$ and using the photoelastic relation $\rho\,\partial\varepsilon_r/\partial\rho = n^4 p$ (with $p$ the photoelastic constant),
$$
\alpha_{R,d} = \frac{8\pi^3}{3\lambda^4}\,n^8 p^2\,k_B T_f\,\kappa_T(T_f),
$$
which is exactly the density-fluctuation term of Kanamori's Eq. (3) {{S:kanamori_ieice_2021}}. A doped core adds a second, independent contribution $A_c$ from concentration fluctuations of the dopant, so $A_R = A_d + A_c$.

Two engineering conclusions follow directly. Rayleigh loss is proportional to $T_f$, so anything that lowers the fictive temperature lowers the loss; and a core with no dopant has no $A_c$. The measured coefficients bear this out. Bulk silica, with $T_f$ usually below that of drawn fiber, has $A_R$ = {{L:ch01.phys.rayleigh_coeff_bulk_silica}}; drawn fibers, whose fictive temperature is about {{L:ch01.phys.fictive_temp_fiber}}, have {{L:ch01.phys.rayleigh_coeff_fiber}}. Sakr and coauthors give {{L:ch01.phys.rayleigh_coeff_psc_record}} for record pure-silica-core fibers made by a slow "cold" draw and about {{L:ch01.phys.rayleigh_coeff_gedoped}} for high-volume, fast-drawn Ge-doped fiber. The ratio of the two, {{C:ex11_span_budget.psc_vs_ge_pct}}, is the roughly one-third higher Rayleigh loss quoted in the same paper {{Q:ch01.phys.rayleigh_coeff_gedoped}}. This is the physics of the pure-silica-core fiber (纯二氧化硅纤芯光纤): it removes the concentration term, and because the index step is made by down-doping the cladding with fluorine rather than up-doping the core with germania, the core glass can be drawn slowly and cold, lowering $T_f$. The same physics underlies ultra-low-loss fiber (超低损耗光纤) products in general.

## Chromatic dispersion

### Taylor expansion of the propagation constant

A guided mode at angular frequency $\omega$ propagates as $e^{i\beta(\omega)z}$. A modulated signal occupies a band around the carrier $\omega_0$ that is narrow compared with $\omega_0$, so we expand
$$
\beta(\omega) = \beta_0 + \beta_1(\omega-\omega_0) + \frac{\beta_2}{2}(\omega-\omega_0)^2 + \frac{\beta_3}{6}(\omega-\omega_0)^3 + \cdots, \qquad \beta_k = \left.\frac{d^k\beta}{d\omega^k}\right|_{\omega_0}.
$$
$\beta_1 = 1/v_g$ is the inverse group velocity: an envelope travels a length $L$ in the group delay $\tau_g = \beta_1 L$. Writing $\beta = n_{\mathrm{eff}}\omega/c$ gives $\beta_1 = (n_{\mathrm{eff}} + \omega\, dn_{\mathrm{eff}}/d\omega)/c = n_g/c$, which defines the group index $n_g$. $\beta_2 = d\beta_1/d\omega$ is the group velocity dispersion (群速度色散, GVD): the rate at which the group delay changes with frequency. $\beta_3$ is the third-order dispersion, the frequency derivative of the GVD.

### From β₂ to D, and the slope S

Engineers measure delay versus *wavelength*, not frequency. The chromatic dispersion (色散) parameter is the group delay per unit length per unit wavelength, $D = d\beta_1/d\lambda$. With $\omega = 2\pi c/\lambda$, $d\omega/d\lambda = -2\pi c/\lambda^2$, so by the chain rule
$$
D = \frac{d\beta_1}{d\omega}\,\frac{d\omega}{d\lambda} = -\frac{2\pi c}{\lambda^2}\,\beta_2 ,
$$
which is the standard relation {{S:gharbi_jphot_2019}}. A positive $D$ ("anomalous" dispersion) means $\beta_2<0$: longer wavelengths are slower. The dispersion slope is $S = dD/d\lambda$. Differentiating the product,
$$
S = \frac{d}{d\lambda}\!\left(-\frac{2\pi c}{\lambda^2}\beta_2\right) = \frac{4\pi c}{\lambda^3}\beta_2 - \frac{2\pi c}{\lambda^2}\frac{d\beta_2}{d\omega}\frac{d\omega}{d\lambda} = \frac{4\pi c}{\lambda^3}\beta_2 + \left(\frac{2\pi c}{\lambda^2}\right)^{2}\beta_3 ,
$$
in agreement with the form given in {{S:vijayan_lic_thesis_2019}} (a thesis; the derivation above is self-contained). For standard fiber in the C-band, the ITU-T representative link value is $D$ = {{L:ch01.cd.g652_link_typ_d1550}}; the conversion gives $\beta_2$ = {{C:ex12_power_fading.beta2_1550}}, which matches the SSMF parameter set used in the Essiambre capacity study {{Q:ch01.kerr.gamma_ssmf_essiambre_params}}.

### Material and waveguide dispersion

The total $D$ of a fiber is, to good approximation, the sum of a material term from the frequency dependence of the glass index and a waveguide term from the frequency dependence of the mode's confinement. For a plane wave in bulk glass, $\beta = n(\lambda)\,2\pi/\lambda$. Then
$$
\beta_1 = \frac{1}{c}\left(n - \lambda\frac{dn}{d\lambda}\right) = \frac{n_g}{c}, \qquad D_M = \frac{d\beta_1}{d\lambda} = \frac{1}{c}\left(\frac{dn}{d\lambda} - \frac{dn}{d\lambda} - \lambda\frac{d^2n}{d\lambda^2}\right) = -\frac{\lambda}{c}\frac{d^2 n}{d\lambda^2}.
$$
Malitson's three-term Sellmeier fit for fused silica {{S:malitson_josa_1965}},
$$
n^2 - 1 = \sum_{j=1}^{3}\frac{B_j\lambda^2}{\lambda^2-\lambda_j^2},
$$
with two ultraviolet resonances and one infrared resonance, reproduces his own tabulated index (for example {{L:ch01.cd.malitson_n_1529nm}} near the C-band) to the precision of the table. Differentiating it numerically, we find that the material dispersion of pure silica vanishes at {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam_zd_material}}, is {{C:ex12_power_fading.dm_1310}} at {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam1310}}, and is {{C:ex12_power_fading.dm_1550}} at {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam1550}}. The bulk group index at {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam1550}} is {{C:ex12_power_fading.ng_bulk_1550}}, close to the effective group index Corning gives for SMF-28 Ultra, {{L:ch01.fiber.smf28ultra_group_index_1550}}; the difference reflects germania doping and the waveguide contribution.

The waveguide term is negative in a step-index fiber. As frequency decreases, the mode spreads into the lower-index cladding and its effective index falls faster than the material index alone would predict, which adds normal dispersion. The difference between the typical fiber value and the material value at {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam1550}} gives $D_W \approx$ {{C:ex12_power_fading.dw_1550}}. Adding a waveguide term of this size shifts the zero from the material value near {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam_zd_material}} to the range ITU-T specifies for G.652.D, $\lambda_0$ = {{L:ch01.cd.g652d_lambda0_range}}. This is the zero-dispersion wavelength that defines the O-band's usefulness for IM/DD. Dispersion-shifted fiber (G.653) pushes $D_W$ much further negative to move $\lambda_0$ into the C-band; we shall see in Section 1.4 why that was a mistake for WDM.

ITU-T approximates the dispersion of G.652 fiber near $\lambda_0$ by {{S:itu_t_g652_2024}}
$$
D(\lambda) = \frac{S_0\,\lambda}{4}\left[1-\left(\frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda}\right)^{4}\right],
$$
where $S_0$ is the slope at $\lambda_0$, specified as {{L:ch01.cd.g652d_s0_range}}. (Differentiate: $S(\lambda) = \tfrac{S_0}{4}[1+3(\lambda_0/\lambda)^4]$, which equals $S_0$ at $\lambda_0$.) In the C-band the specification bounds are {{L:ch01.cd.g652d_d1550_range}}. The O-band is where the formula matters most for system design. With $\lambda_0$ at either edge of its range and $S_0$ at its maximum, $D$ at {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam1310}} lies between {{C:ex12_power_fading.d1310_neg}} and {{C:ex12_power_fading.d1310_pos}}, so the worst-case magnitude is {{C:ex12_power_fading.d1310_worst}}, more than an order of magnitude below the C-band value. At the short edge of the 400G-FR4 grid, {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam1264}}, the same formula gives {{C:ex12_power_fading.d1264_curve}}, which coincides with the statistical lower boundary that the 2024 edition of G.652 derives from a multi-vendor survey for a single cable, {{L:ch01.cd.g652_oband_link_dmin_m1}}.

### Dispersion length

Neglecting loss and nonlinearity, each frequency component of the envelope acquires the phase $\beta_2\Omega^2 z/2$, where $\Omega = \omega-\omega_0$, so that in the frame moving with the group velocity, $\tilde A(z,\Omega) = \tilde A(0,\Omega)\,e^{i\beta_2\Omega^2 z/2}$. For a Gaussian input $A(0,T) = \sqrt{P_0}\,e^{-T^2/2T_0^2}$, the spectrum is Gaussian, the quadratic phase can be absorbed into a complex width, and the inverse transform gives
$$
|A(z,T)|^2 = \frac{P_0}{\sqrt{1+(z/L_D)^2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{T^2}{T_0^2\,(1+(z/L_D)^2)}\right], \qquad L_D = \frac{T_0^2}{|\beta_2|}.
$$
The dispersion length $L_D$ is the distance at which the pulse width has grown by $\sqrt2$. If we take $T_0$ equal to the symbol period of a {{C:ex12_power_fading.rs_gbd}} lane, which is {{C:ex12_power_fading.tsym_ps}}, we find $L_D$ = {{C:ex12_power_fading.ld_1550_km}} at {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam1550}} but {{C:ex12_power_fading.ld_1310_km}} at {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam1310}} for the worst-case O-band dispersion. A coherent receiver, which detects the field, can undo any amount of dispersion; a direct-detection receiver cannot.

### IM/DD power fading

A direct-detection receiver measures $|E|^2$ and discards the optical phase. Consider a chirp-free intensity modulation with small index $m$ at baseband frequency $f$, $\Omega_m = 2\pi f$. The field is $E(0,t) = \sqrt{P_0}\,[1+m\cos\Omega_m t]^{1/2} \approx \sqrt{P_0}\,[1+\tfrac{m}{2}\cos\Omega_m t]$, a carrier with two sidebands of amplitude $m\sqrt{P_0}/4$ at $\pm\Omega_m$. After a length $L$ each sideband has acquired the phase $\phi = \beta_2\Omega_m^2 L/2$ relative to the carrier; because the phase is quadratic in frequency, both sidebands get the *same* phase. Dropping the common $\beta_1$ delay,
$$
E(L,t) = \sqrt{P_0}\left[1 + \frac{m}{4}e^{i\phi}\left(e^{i\Omega_m t}+e^{-i\Omega_m t}\right)\right] = \sqrt{P_0}\left[1+\frac{m}{2}e^{i\phi}\cos\Omega_m t\right].
$$
The detected power, to first order in $m$, is
$$
|E(L,t)|^2 \approx P_0\left[1 + m\cos\phi\,\cos\Omega_m t\right],
$$
so the small-signal transfer function of the fiber for intensity is {{S:zhu_electronics_2021}} {{S:ouyang_jlt_2019}}
$$
H(f) = \cos\!\left(\frac{\beta_2\omega^2 L}{2}\right)\bigg|_{\omega=2\pi f} = \cos\!\left(2\pi^2\beta_2 f^2 L\right) = \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi\lambda^2 D L f^2}{c}\right).
$$
The two sidebands beat with the carrier with opposite-sign phase errors, and their contributions cancel when $\phi = \pi/2$. The first power-fading null is at
$$
f_1 = \sqrt{\frac{c}{2\lambda^2|D|L}} .
$$
Because $f_1 \propto (|D|L)^{-1/2}$, a twenty-fold larger dispersion costs a factor of about four and a half in null frequency, or a twenty-fold shorter reach at fixed null frequency.

**Result.** For chirp-free IM/DD, the fiber acts as the filter $|H(f)| = |\cos(2\pi^2\beta_2 f^2 L)|$. Requiring the first null to lie above the Nyquist frequency $R_s/2$ gives the reach $L_{\max} = 2c/(\lambda^2|D|R_s^2)$; requiring $|H(R_s/2)|\geq1/\sqrt2$ (half the electrical power) halves it.
:::

## Polarization effects: PMD and PDL

### Birefringence and the principal states

A nominally circular core supports two degenerate polarization modes. Real fibers have small, random core ellipticity and frozen-in stress, so the two modes have slightly different propagation constants, $\Delta\beta = \beta_x-\beta_y$, along local axes whose orientation varies along the fiber. Marcuse, Menyuk and Wai give the characteristic scales for communication fibers as beat lengths and orientation-correlation lengths of metres to hundreds of metres, much shorter than the dispersive and nonlinear lengths of hundreds of kilometres {{L:ch01.manakov.length_scales|v}}.

For a short uniform section of length $\ell$ the two modes arrive with a differential group delay (差分群时延, DGD) $\delta\tau = \ell\,d(\Delta\beta)/d\omega$. For a long fiber with random orientation the picture is subtler, but one can show that at any frequency there exist two orthogonal input polarizations, the *principal states of polarization* (PSPs), whose output polarization is independent of frequency to first order, and which emerge with a delay difference $\Delta\tau$, the DGD of the fiber. The PMD vector is $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \Delta\tau\,\hat{\mathbf{p}}$, with $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ the PSP direction on the Poincaré sphere. Polarization mode dispersion (偏振模色散, PMD) is the statistics of this vector.

### Why the mean DGD grows as √L

Model the fiber as $N$ short sections, each a linear retarder with its own PMD vector $\boldsymbol{\tau}_k$ of fixed magnitude $\delta\tau$. The concatenation rule for PMD vectors is $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{tot}} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_N + R_N\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{tot},N-1}$, where $R_N$ is the $3\times3$ rotation that section $N$ applies on the Poincaré sphere. Unrolling,
$$
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{tot}} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \left(R_N R_{N-1}\cdots R_{k+1}\right)\boldsymbol{\tau}_k .
$$
If the section orientations are random and independent, the rotated vectors are independent and isotropically distributed with zero mean. The squared magnitude then adds like a random walk,
$$
\langle|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{tot}}|^2\rangle = \sum_k \langle|\boldsymbol{\tau}_k|^2\rangle + \sum_{j\neq k}\langle \boldsymbol{\tau}'_j\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}'_k\rangle = N\,\delta\tau^2 ,
$$
so the RMS DGD grows as $\sqrt N\propto\sqrt L$. This is the random-walk scaling Gordon and Kogelnik describe {{Q:ch01.pmd.dgd_sqrt_length}}. It is why the PMD coefficient of a randomly coupled fiber is quoted in ps/√km and the mean DGD of a link is
$$
\langle\Delta\tau\rangle = D_{\mathrm{PMD}}\sqrt{L},
$$
the definition used by ITU-T {{S:itu_t_g650_2_2015}}. Gordon and Kogelnik give a typical range of {{L:ch01.pmd.dgd_sqrt_length}} for the mean DGD of a {{C:ex13_pmd_outage.gk_len}} link, depending on fiber type.

### The Maxwellian distribution

By the central limit theorem, each Cartesian component of $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{tot}}$ is Gaussian with zero mean and the same variance $\sigma^2 = N\delta\tau^2/3$. The magnitude of an isotropic three-dimensional Gaussian vector follows the Maxwell distribution, which is how Foschini and Poole established the Maxwellian DGD law {{S:foschini_poole_jlt_1991}}. In spherical coordinates the probability of finding $|\boldsymbol{\tau}|$ in $[x, x+dx]$ is $4\pi x^2 (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-3/2}e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}dx$. Its mean is $\langle\Delta\tau\rangle = 2\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}$ and its RMS is $\sigma\sqrt3$. Eliminating $\sigma$ in favour of the mean gives the form quoted by Reimer {{S:reimer_msc_thesis_2007}},
$$
p(\Delta\tau) = \frac{32\,\Delta\tau^2}{\pi^2\langle\Delta\tau\rangle^3}\exp\!\left(-\frac{4\,\Delta\tau^2}{\pi\langle\Delta\tau\rangle^2}\right),
$$
and the ratio of RMS to mean,
$$
\frac{\langle\Delta\tau^2\rangle^{1/2}}{\langle\Delta\tau\rangle} = \frac{\sigma\sqrt3}{2\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}} = \left(\frac{3\pi}{8}\right)^{1/2},
$$
which ITU-T G.650.2 gives as its Eq. (3-3) for randomly mode-coupled fiber {{S:itu_t_g650_2_2015}}. Numerically the ratio is {{C:ex13_pmd_outage.rms_over_mean}}.

The tail probability is what matters for outage. With $a = \sigma$, integration by parts gives
$$
P(\Delta\tau > x) = \mathrm{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{a\sqrt2}\right) + \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,\frac{x}{a}\,e^{-x^2/2a^2}, \qquad a = \langle\Delta\tau\rangle\sqrt{\pi/8}.
$$
At three times the mean this gives {{C:ex13_pmd_outage.p_ratio3}}, and at three and a half times the mean {{C:ex13_pmd_outage.p_ratio35}}, reproducing the values tabulated in ITU-T G.959.1, {{L:ch01.pmd.g959_ratio_3p0_prob}} and {{L:ch01.pmd.g959_ratio_3p5_prob}}.

![Maxwellian DGD probability density computed from the closed-form pdf for three link lengths, with the PMD coefficient equal to the G.652.D link design value, and the 400G-FR4 DGD tolerance (computed for this book, not reproduced).](figs/ch01/maxwellian_dgd.svg){width=90%}

### Link design value $\mathrm{PMD}_Q$

A cable manufacturer cannot know which reels will be concatenated into a link, so ITU-T specifies a statistical bound. $\mathrm{PMD}_Q$ is the value that the PMD coefficient of $M$ randomly selected, concatenated cable sections exceeds with probability $Q$; the G.652 tables use $M$ = 20 and $Q$ = {{L:ch01.pmd.pmdq_definition|v}} {{S:itu_t_g650_2_2015}}. For G.652.D the limit is {{L:ch01.pmd.g652d_pmdq}}. $\mathrm{PMD}_Q$ bounds the *coefficient*, so a link built from good cable may sit far below it, and the bound depends on $M$: G.652 gives a normalized $\mathrm{PMD}_Q$ of {{L:ch01.pmd.g652_pmdq_vs_m|v}} for $M$ = 4 relative to $M$ = 20, as an illustration.

### Second-order PMD, PDL, and what DSP can remove

The PMD vector varies with frequency. Expanding $\boldsymbol{\tau}(\omega) = \boldsymbol{\tau}(\omega_0) + \boldsymbol{\tau}_\omega(\omega-\omega_0)$, the derivative $\boldsymbol{\tau}_\omega$ has two components: one parallel to $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$, $d(\Delta\tau)/d\omega$, which acts like a polarization-dependent chromatic dispersion, and one perpendicular, $\Delta\tau\,d\hat{\mathbf{p}}/d\omega$, which describes PSP depolarization across the signal band. This is second-order PMD. For IM/DD receivers it adds pulse distortion that first-order DGD tolerances do not capture.

Polarization dependent loss (偏振相关损耗, PDL) is a different animal: it is a *non-unitary* effect. Isolators, couplers and amplifier components attenuate one polarization more than the orthogonal one. In Jones-matrix language, PMD is a frequency-dependent unitary matrix $U(\omega)$, while a PDL element is a Hermitian matrix with unequal eigenvalues. This distinction determines what a coherent receiver can do. A coherent receiver detects both polarizations of the field and applies an adaptive $2\times2$ MIMO equalizer (Chapter 2). Any unitary $U(\omega)$ can be inverted exactly, so PMD of all orders is removed provided the equalizer's impulse response is long compared with the DGD. A PDL element, however, attenuates signal and in-line ASE noise differently; inverting it restores the signal but amplifies the noise in the weak polarization, so the SNR loss is not recoverable by linear equalization. We did not find a sourced quantification of PDL magnitudes or of the PDL-induced SNR penalty, and so we give none (see the needs file).

## Kerr nonlinearity and the NLSE

### The intensity-dependent index

Silica has inversion symmetry, so its lowest-order nonlinear susceptibility is $\chi^{(3)}$. For a quasi-monochromatic field its dominant effect is the optical Kerr effect (克尔效应): the refractive index depends on the local intensity $I$,
$$
n = n_0 + n_2 I ,
$$
as ITU-T G.Sup47 states for intense fields in silica fibers {{S:itu_t_gsup47_2025}}. The coefficient $n_2$ is tiny, of order $10^{-20}\,\mathrm{m^2/W}$, but the fiber compensates with two enormous factors: a mode area of order a hundred square micrometres, and interaction lengths of tens of kilometres. Agrawal estimates that the product of intensity and effective length available in a low-loss fiber exceeds that of a bulk medium by a factor of order {{L:ch01.kerr.fiber_enhancement_factor|v}} in the C-band.

Measured values of $n_2$ disagree at the level of tens of percent, and the disagreement correlates with method:

| Pure-silica core | {{L:ch01.kerr.n2_kato1995_pscf}} | XPM, depolarized pump |
| Dispersion-shifted | {{L:ch01.kerr.n2_kato1995_dsf}} | XPM, depolarized pump |

G.Sup47 summarizes the composition dependence with a linear empirical law, $n_2 = 0.0556\,\eta_{\mathrm{GeO_2}} + 2.61$ (in units of $10^{-20}\,\mathrm{m^2/W}$, with $\eta_{\mathrm{GeO_2}}$ the effective germania concentration in mol%); germania raises $n_2$ because, by Kim's estimate, its own $n_2$ is about {{L:ch01.kerr.n2_germania_ratio_kim1994|v}} times that of silica. In this chapter we use the G.Sup47 pure-silica constant, {{L:ch01.kerr.n2_pure_silica_gsup47}}, as the central value; for a Ge-doped core it is a lower bound.

### From n₂ to γ

The mode is not uniform, so we need the modal average of the index change. First-order perturbation theory for the propagation constant gives
$$
\delta\beta = k_0\,\frac{\iint \delta n\,|F(x,y)|^2\,dx\,dy}{\iint |F(x,y)|^2\,dx\,dy}, \qquad \delta n = n_2 I(x,y) = n_2\,\frac{P\,|F|^2}{\iint |F|^2\,dx\,dy},
$$
where $F(x,y)$ is the transverse mode profile and $k_0 = \omega_0/c$. Substituting,
$$
\delta\beta = \frac{n_2\omega_0}{c}\,P\,\frac{\iint |F|^4\,dx\,dy}{\left(\iint |F|^2\,dx\,dy\right)^2} \equiv \gamma P, \qquad \gamma = \frac{n_2\omega_0}{c\,A_{\mathrm{eff}}}, \qquad A_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{\left(\iint |F|^2\,dx\,dy\right)^2}{\iint |F|^4\,dx\,dy},
$$
which is Agrawal's definition {{S:agrawal_josab_2011}}. Equivalently $\gamma = 2\pi n_2/(\lambda A_{\mathrm{eff}})$. Agrawal gives {{L:ch01.kerr.gamma_range_silica_agrawal}} as the range for most silica fibers.

### Deriving the scalar NLSE

Write the field as $E = F(x,y)A(z,t)e^{i(\beta_0 z-\omega_0 t)}$, with $|A|^2$ the power. In the frequency domain each spectral component of the envelope propagates with $\beta(\omega) + \gamma|A|^2 + i\alpha/2$, where the imaginary part accounts for power loss $e^{-\alpha z}$. Thus
$$
\frac{\partial\tilde A}{\partial z} = i\left[\beta(\omega)-\beta_0 + \gamma|A|^2\right]\tilde A - \frac{\alpha}{2}\tilde A .
$$
Expand $\beta(\omega)$ as in Section 1.2 and return to the time domain with the replacement $\omega-\omega_0 \to i\,\partial/\partial t$ (which follows from our convention $e^{-i\omega t}$). The term $\beta_1(\omega-\omega_0)$ becomes $i\beta_1\partial_t$, and $\tfrac12\beta_2(\omega-\omega_0)^2$ becomes $-\tfrac12\beta_2\partial_t^2$. Collecting terms,
$$
\frac{\partial A}{\partial z} + \frac{\alpha}{2}A + \beta_1\frac{\partial A}{\partial t} + \frac{i\beta_2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 A}{\partial t^2} = i\gamma|A|^2A ,
$$
which is Agrawal's Eq. (10) {{S:agrawal_josab_2011}}. Moving to the retarded time $T = t-\beta_1 z$ removes the group-delay term and gives the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLSE) with loss,
$$
i\frac{\partial A}{\partial z} + \frac{i\alpha}{2}A - \frac{\beta_2}{2}\frac{\partial^2 A}{\partial T^2} + \gamma|A|^2A = 0 ,
$$
Agrawal's Eq. (11). Keeping the cubic term of the expansion adds $-\tfrac{\beta_3}{6}\partial_T^3 A$ to the left side of the first form; this is our own specialization of Agrawal's general Eq. (7), which also includes the delayed Raman response and self-steepening, both negligible for the multi-gigabaud signals of this book.

### Effective length and nonlinear length

Set $\beta_2 = 0$ for a moment. Then $A(z,T) = A(0,T)\,e^{-\alpha z/2}e^{i\phi_{\mathrm{NL}}(z,T)}$ with
$$
\frac{\partial\phi_{\mathrm{NL}}}{\partial z} = \gamma|A(0,T)|^2e^{-\alpha z} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \phi_{\mathrm{NL}}(L,T) = \gamma P(0,T)\int_0^L e^{-\alpha z}\,dz = \gamma P(0,T)\,\frac{1-e^{-\alpha L}}{\alpha}.
$$
The integral defines the effective length
$$
L_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{1-e^{-\alpha L}}{\alpha} \;\xrightarrow{\;\alpha L\gg 1\;}\; L_{\mathrm{eff,a}} = \frac{1}{\alpha},
$$
the length of lossless fiber that would produce the same nonlinear phase {{S:agrawal_josab_2011}}. The nonlinearity is concentrated in the first few tens of kilometres after each amplifier, where the power is high; this fact is what makes the GN model's per-span accounting work (Section 1.6). G.Sup47 gives $L_{\mathrm{eff,a}}$ = {{L:ch01.kerr.leff_asymptote_gsup47}} for a fiber of {{C:ex14_nonlinear_lengths.fwm_alpha_db}}; the unrounded value is {{C:ex14_nonlinear_lengths.leffa_025}}, the difference coming from G.Sup47's rounding of $\alpha$. The nonlinear length $L_{\mathrm{NL}} = 1/(\gamma P)$ is the distance over which the nonlinear phase reaches one radian in a lossless fiber, so the peak nonlinear phase after one span is $\phi_{\mathrm{NL}} = L_{\mathrm{eff}}/L_{\mathrm{NL}}$ {{S:agrawal_josab_2011}}. Comparing $L_{\mathrm{NL}}$ with the dispersion length $L_D$ of Section 1.2, Agrawal's soliton number $N^2 = L_D/L_{\mathrm{NL}} = \gamma P_0T_0^2/|\beta_2|$ measures which effect dominates.

### SPM, XPM, and FWM

When several WDM channels share the fiber, write $A = \sum_j A_j e^{-i\Omega_j T}$ and substitute into $|A|^2A$. The products sort into three families.

* **Self-phase modulation** (自相位调制, SPM): terms $|A_j|^2A_j$, a phase on channel $j$ proportional to its own power. With dispersion, the time-varying phase converts into amplitude distortion.
* **Cross-phase modulation** (交叉相位调制, XPM): terms $2|A_k|^2A_j$, $k\neq j$. The factor 2 is combinatorial: the product $A_kA_k^*A_j$ arises from two orderings in the expansion of $|A|^2A$. XPM imprints the power fluctuations of every other channel onto channel $j$'s phase.
* **Four-wave mixing** (四波混频, FWM): terms $A_kA_lA_m^*$ with $\Omega_k+\Omega_l-\Omega_m$ equal to a channel frequency $\Omega_F$. These transfer power to new frequencies.

FWM is a parametric process, so it is efficient only if the generated wave stays in phase with its driving polarization. Expanding $\beta$ about $\omega_m$ to second order, with $\omega_k = \omega_m + a$, $\omega_l = \omega_m + b$ and $\omega_F = \omega_m + a + b$,
$$
\Delta\beta = \beta(\omega_F) + \beta(\omega_m) - \beta(\omega_k) - \beta(\omega_l) = \frac{\beta_2}{2}\left[(a+b)^2 - a^2 - b^2\right] = \beta_2\,(\omega_k-\omega_m)(\omega_l-\omega_m).
$$
The linear terms cancel exactly, so only GVD causes phase mismatch. The FWM field generated along a lossy span is $\propto\int_0^L e^{-\alpha z}e^{i\Delta\beta z}\,dz$, and normalizing its squared magnitude to the phase-matched value $L_{\mathrm{eff}}^2$ gives the efficiency that Poggiolini and coauthors use as the kernel of the GN model {{S:poggiolini_jlt_2012}}:
$$
\eta_{\mathrm{FWM}} = \frac{1}{L_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}\left|\frac{1-e^{-\alpha L}e^{i\Delta\beta L}}{\alpha - i\Delta\beta}\right|^2 \approx \frac{\alpha^2}{\alpha^2+\Delta\beta^2}\quad(\alpha L\gg1).
$$
Once $|\Delta\beta|\gg\alpha$ the efficiency falls as $\Delta\beta^{-2}$, that is, as $(D\,\Delta f^2)^{-2}$.

![Degenerate FWM efficiency versus channel spacing, computed from the phase-mismatch formula for the span of the G.Sup47 example at several dispersion values (computed for this book, not reproduced).](figs/ch01/fwm_efficiency.svg){width=90%}

This is the physics behind the history of dispersion-shifted fiber. G.653 fiber places $\lambda_0$ in the C-band, so WDM channels there see almost no phase mismatch and FWM builds up coherently. ITU-T states that WDM systems over G.652 and G.655 fiber suffer much less FWM than over G.653 {{Q:ch01.fwm.g653_vs_g652_g655_gsup47}}, and the original rationale for G.655 non-zero dispersion-shifted fiber was precisely that a small but non-zero $|D|$ across the band suppresses FWM {{Q:ch01.fwm.g655_scope_1996}}; the 1996 edition bounded $|D|$ in the C-band to {{L:ch01.fwm.g655_dmin_dmax_1996}}. The local dispersion matters, not only the average: G.655 warns that longitudinal variations can bring the local dispersion near zero {{Q:ch01.fwm.local_dispersion_note_g655}}, and G.Sup47 reports that random dispersion variations reduced FWM crosstalk in a G.653 link by {{L:ch01.fwm.nonuniform_dispersion_10db}} compared with uniform dispersion.

### The Manakov equation

The scalar NLSE ignores polarization. For the Jones vector $\mathbf{A} = (A_x, A_y)^{T}$ in a linearly birefringent fiber, the isotropic $\chi^{(3)}$ tensor gives the Kerr term
$$
\gamma\left[\left(|A_x|^2 + \tfrac23|A_y|^2\right)A_x + \tfrac13 A_y^2A_x^*\right]
$$
for the $x$ component (and symmetrically for $y$). Because the birefringence axes rotate randomly on length scales of metres to hundreds of metres, while nonlinear effects build over tens of kilometres, the polarization state explores the whole Poincaré sphere many times within a nonlinear length. Averaging the Kerr term uniformly over the sphere gives $\tfrac89\gamma|\mathbf{A}|^2\mathbf{A}$, with $|\mathbf{A}|^2 = |A_x|^2+|A_y|^2$. Marcuse, Menyuk and Wai derived this "Manakov-PMD" equation with the well-known factor {{L:ch01.manakov.eight_ninths|v}} {{S:marcuse_jlt_1997}}; on its right side remain a linear PMD term and a nonlinear PMD term, the latter negligible for practical communication parameters {{Q:ch01.manakov.nonlinear_pmd_negligible}}. Dropping both and writing it in our sign convention,
$$
i\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial z} + \frac{i\alpha}{2}\mathbf{A} - \frac{\beta_2}{2}\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{A}}{\partial T^2} + \frac{8}{9}\gamma|\mathbf{A}|^2\mathbf{A} = 0 ,
$$
the Manakov equation. It is the starting point of dual-polarization nonlinear models such as the GN model of Section 1.6.

## Optical amplification and ASE noise

### The erbium-doped fiber amplifier

The erbium-doped fiber amplifier (掺铒光纤放大器, EDFA) is a few metres of silica fiber doped with Er³⁺ ions, pumped at a shorter wavelength to invert the population of the $^4I_{13/2}$ manifold relative to the ground manifold. Its gain band defines the C-band, which ITU-T gives as {{L:ch01.amp.cband_range_itu}} {{Q:ch01.amp.cband_range_itu}}; L-band amplifiers operate over {{L:ch01.amp.lband_range_itu}}.

### The ASE power spectral density

Consider one optical mode (one polarization, one frequency bin) propagating through a medium with upper-level population $N_2$ and lower-level population $N_1$. Let $\bar n(z)$ be the mean photon number in the mode. Stimulated emission adds photons at a rate proportional to $N_2\bar n$, absorption removes them at a rate proportional to $N_1\bar n$, and spontaneous emission into the mode adds them at a rate proportional to $N_2$ with the *same* coefficient as stimulated emission (Einstein's relation: spontaneous emission into a mode is emission stimulated by the vacuum, one photon's worth). Thus
$$
\frac{d\bar n}{dz} = g\,(\bar n + n_{\mathrm{sp}}), \qquad g \propto N_2-N_1, \qquad n_{\mathrm{sp}} = \frac{N_2}{N_2-N_1}.
$$
The spontaneous emission factor (自发辐射因子) $n_{\mathrm{sp}}\geq1$ tends to one at full inversion {{Q:ch01.amp.nsp_unity_full_inversion}}. Integrating with $G = e^{\int g\,dz}$,
$$
\bar n(L) = G\,\bar n(0) + n_{\mathrm{sp}}\,(G-1).
$$
The second term is amplified spontaneous emission (放大自发辐射, ASE): even with no input, the output contains $n_{\mathrm{sp}}(G-1)$ photons per mode. A mode of bandwidth $\delta\nu$ carries photons at a rate $\delta\nu$ per photon of occupation, so the ASE power per unit bandwidth in one polarization is {{S:carney_oe_2013}}
$$
S_{\mathrm{ASE}} = n_{\mathrm{sp}}\,h\nu\,(G-1).
$$
In both polarizations the PSD is twice this, $2S_{\mathrm{ASE}}$ {{S:desurvire_scholarpedia_optamp}}.

### Noise figure and its quantum limit

The noise factor $F$, whose decibel form is the noise figure (噪声系数), is defined, following IEC 61291-1, as the ratio of the electrical SNR of the detected photocurrent at the amplifier input (with a shot-noise-limited input) to that at the output {{S:baney_gallion_tucker_oft_2000}}:
$$
F = \frac{\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{in}}}{\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{out}}}, \qquad \mathrm{NF} = 10\log_{10}F .
$$
At the input, an ideal detector of responsivity $\Re = e/h\nu$ (unit quantum efficiency) receiving power $P_{\mathrm{in}}$ sees the photocurrent $I = \Re P_{\mathrm{in}}$ with shot noise $2eIB_e$ in electrical bandwidth $B_e$, so $\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{in}} = I^2/(2eIB_e) = P_{\mathrm{in}}/(2h\nu B_e)$. At the output the signal current is $\Re GP_{\mathrm{in}}$. For high gain the dominant noise is the beat between the signal and the co-polarized ASE: a field $\sqrt{GP_{\mathrm{in}}}$ beating with ASE of PSD $S_{\mathrm{ASE}}$ produces current noise of PSD $4\Re^2GP_{\mathrm{in}}S_{\mathrm{ASE}}$ (two-sided ASE about the carrier folds onto the baseband), giving variance $4\Re^2GP_{\mathrm{in}}S_{\mathrm{ASE}}B_e$. The amplified signal's own shot noise adds $2e\Re GP_{\mathrm{in}}B_e$. Then
$$
\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{out}} = \frac{\Re^2G^2P_{\mathrm{in}}^2}{4\Re^2GP_{\mathrm{in}}S_{\mathrm{ASE}}B_e + 2e\Re GP_{\mathrm{in}}B_e}
$$
and
$$
F = \frac{P_{\mathrm{in}}}{2h\nu B_e}\cdot\frac{4GS_{\mathrm{ASE}}B_e + 2h\nu GB_e}{G^2P_{\mathrm{in}}} = \frac{2S_{\mathrm{ASE}}}{h\nu G}+\frac1G = \frac{2n_{\mathrm{sp}}(G-1)}{G}+\frac1G ,
$$
which is Carney's Eq. (13) {{S:carney_oe_2013}}. For $G\gg1$, $F\to2n_{\mathrm{sp}}$ {{Q:ch01.amp.nf_high_gain_2nsp}}, and with complete inversion the noise figure is {{L:ch01.amp.nf_quantum_limit_3db_carney}}. The limit is not an artifact of erbium: Caves showed that any high-gain, phase-insensitive linear amplifier must add, referred to its input, at least the half-quantum of zero-point fluctuation {{Q:ch01.amp.caves_half_quantum_limit}}, which in the optical noise-figure definition is $F\geq2$ {{Q:ch01.amp.nf_min_3db_desurvire}}. The factor of two has a clean interpretation: a phase-insensitive amplifier amplifies both quadratures equally, so it must add noise in both, while the input shot noise is the vacuum noise of one.

Real amplifiers sit above the limit. Datasheet values include {{L:ch01.amp.accelink_mw_nf_max}} for an Accelink multichannel module at its rated gain, {{L:ch01.amp.accelink_vga_nf}} for an Accelink variable-gain card at its highest gain (rising at lower gain), {{L:ch01.amp.coherent_pols_tx_nf}} and {{L:ch01.amp.coherent_pols_rx_nf}} for the booster and preamplifier of a pluggable line system, and {{L:ch01.amp.viavi_lownoise_nf}} for a low-noise single-channel test amplifier at small input (all datasheet values). For the line amplifiers of this chapter we use the Accelink value. A finite-gain detail is worth noting: at $n_{\mathrm{sp}}$ = 1 and the gain of our span, $F = 2 - 1/G$ gives {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.nf_ideal}}, fractionally *below* $10\log_{10}2$, because the $1/G$ shot-noise term is counted in the definition.

### OSNR after a chain of amplifiers

The optical signal-to-noise ratio (光信噪比, OSNR) compares the signal power to the ASE power in a reference bandwidth $B_{\mathrm{ref}}$, counting noise in *both* polarizations whether or not the signal is polarization-multiplexed {{S:essiambre_tkach_procieee_2012}}:
$$
\mathrm{OSNR} = \frac{P}{2S_{\mathrm{ASE}}B_{\mathrm{ref}}}.
$$
The reference bandwidth is conventionally {{L:ch01.osnr.gsup39_bref_typical}}, as in ITU-T G.697 monitoring and IEC in-band OSNR definitions {{Q:ch01.osnr.bref_g697_0p1nm}} {{Q:ch01.osnr.bref_iec_tr61282_12}}. In frequency this is $\Delta\nu = c\,\Delta\lambda/\lambda^2$ = {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.bref_ghz}} at {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam1550}}, usually rounded to {{L:ch01.osnr.bref_0p1nm_equals_12p5ghz}}.

Now take a transparent chain of $N_s$ identical spans, each of loss $L_{\mathrm{span}}$ followed by an amplifier of gain $G = L_{\mathrm{span}}$ that restores the launch power $P$. Each amplifier adds the same ASE, and the ASE of earlier amplifiers is attenuated by the next span and restored by the next amplifier, so all contributions arrive at the receiver with unit net gain and add:
$$
\mathrm{OSNR} = \frac{P}{N_s\cdot 2n_{\mathrm{sp}}h\nu(G-1)B_{\mathrm{ref}}} = \frac{P}{N_s\,(FG-1)\,h\nu B_{\mathrm{ref}}} \approx \frac{P}{N_s\,F\,G\,h\nu B_{\mathrm{ref}}},
$$
where we used $2n_{\mathrm{sp}}(G-1) = FG-1$ from the noise-figure expression. In decibels, with $P$ in dBm,
$$
\mathrm{OSNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = P_{\mathrm{dBm}} - L_{\mathrm{span}} - \mathrm{NF} - 10\log_{10}N_s - 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{h\nu B_{\mathrm{ref}}}{P_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right),
$$
This is ITU-T G.Sup39 Eq. (9-19) in the 2006 numbering {{S:itu_t_gsup39_2006}}; the 2025 edition renumbers the clause but keeps the relation {{S:itu_t_gsup39_2025_10}}. If a booster amplifier with gain equal to the span loss precedes the first span, there are $N_s+1$ amplifiers and the logarithm becomes $10\log_{10}(N_s+1)$ (Eq. (9-18)).

### The fifty-eight-decibel rule from $h\nu B_{\mathrm{ref}}$

The last term is a constant. At {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam1550}}, $\nu = c/\lambda$ = {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.nu_thz}}, so $h\nu$ = {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.hnu_j}} using the exact SI values of $h$ = {{L:ch01.const.planck_h}} and $c$ = {{L:ch01.const.speed_of_light_c}}. Multiplying by $B_{\mathrm{ref}}$ = {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.bref_ghz}} gives the power of one photon per second per hertz in the reference band, $h\nu B_{\mathrm{ref}}$, which is {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.hnuB_dbm}}. Hence, with $P_{\mathrm{ref}}$ equal to one milliwatt, $-10\log_{10}(h\nu B_{\mathrm{ref}}/P_{\mathrm{ref}})$ = {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.const58}} (or {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.const58_125}} with the rounded bandwidth), and
$$
\mathrm{OSNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} \approx 58 + P_{\mathrm{dBm}} - L_{\mathrm{span}} - \mathrm{NF} - 10\log_{10}N_s .
$$
This is the fifty-eight-decibel rule, with the constant {{L:ch01.osnr.gsup39_58db_constant}} as G.Sup39 states it; the same source gives the single-preamplifier version, OSNR = 58 + received power − NF, in the same form {{S:itu_t_gsup39_2006}}. A decibel of span loss, noise figure or launch power is a decibel of OSNR, and each doubling of the number of spans costs {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.doubling_db}}. The constant itself changes with wavelength because both $h\nu$ and the frequency width of a fixed wavelength interval scale as $\lambda^{-1}$ and $\lambda^{-2}$; Problem 8 evaluates it in the O-band.

### From OSNR to the electrical SNR of a coherent receiver

A coherent receiver with matched filtering sees noise only in the signal bandwidth, approximately the symbol rate $R_s$ for a Nyquist-shaped signal, not in $B_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Defining the per-symbol SNR as $P/(N_0R_s)$ with $N_0$ the noise PSD summed over the polarizations that carry signal, Essiambre and Tkach derive {{S:essiambre_tkach_procieee_2012}}
$$
\mathrm{OSNR} = \frac{p\,R_s}{2B_{\mathrm{ref}}}\,\mathrm{SNR},
$$
with $p$ = 1 for a single-polarization and $p$ = 2 for a polarization-multiplexed signal. For dual polarization, $\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = \mathrm{OSNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} - 10\log_{10}(R_s/B_{\mathrm{ref}})$. At {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.rs_gbd}} the conversion term is {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.osnr_to_snr_db}}: a coherent receiver's SNR is about seven decibels below the OSNR printed on an optical spectrum analyzer. The SNR, not the OSNR, enters the GN model and the Shannon formula of Section 1.6.

## The GN and EGN models of nonlinear interference

### From waveform distortion to noise

ASE is independent of signal power, so the OSNR improves decibel for decibel with launch power. Kerr distortion grows with the cube of the field and, in a long dispersion-unmanaged coherent link, looks to the receiver like additive noise. Its power is called nonlinear interference (非线性干扰, NLI). The GN ("Gaussian noise") model, developed by Poggiolini and coworkers, turns this observation into a closed-form noise power and thereby into the most-used design rule of modern coherent systems {{S:poggiolini_jlt_2012}}.

### Assumptions

The GN model rests on three assumptions, each with a physical justification.

1. *Gaussian signal.* In an uncompensated link the accumulated dispersion spreads each symbol over hundreds of neighbours, so by the central limit theorem the transmitted field quickly looks like stationary Gaussian noise with the WDM power spectral density $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{WDM}}(f)$. Poggiolini gives about {{L:ch01.gn.gaussianization_distance}} of SMF as the distance over which a signal of about {{C:ex16_gn_optimum.gauss_rs}} approximately Gaussianizes {{Q:ch01.gn.gaussianization_distance}}.
2. *First-order perturbation.* The nonlinearity is weak at practical powers (the per-span phases of Section 1.4 are hundredths of a radian), so the NLI field is computed to first order in $\gamma$ by treating the linear solution as the source of the $|A|^2A$ term.
3. *Span-wise accumulation.* NLI generated in different spans adds in field with a phase set by the accumulated dispersion. If these phases decorrelate quickly, the NLI powers simply add (the "incoherent" GN, or IGN, model).

The model also assumes a transparent, homogeneous link: identical spans, loss exactly compensated by each amplifier, and dual-polarization signals {{Q:ch01.gn.validity_envelope_2012}}.

### The GN reference formula and its closed form

Under these assumptions the NLI PSD at frequency $f$ after one span is {{S:poggiolini_jlt_2012}}
$$
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{NLI}}(f) = \frac{16}{27}\gamma^2L_{\mathrm{eff}}^2\iint \mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{WDM}}(f_1)\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{WDM}}(f_2)\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{WDM}}(f_1+f_2-f)\;\eta_{\mathrm{FWM}}(f_1,f_2,f)\;\chi(f_1,f_2,f)\;df_1\,df_2 .
$$
The structure is transparent once Section 1.4 is understood. Every triple of spectral components at $f_1$, $f_2$ and $f_1+f_2-f$ produces a four-wave-mixing product at $f$. Because the components are independent Gaussian variables, their products add in power, weighted by the product of the three PSDs. The weight is the FWM efficiency of Section 1.4 with phase mismatch $\Delta\beta = 4\pi^2\beta_2(f_1-f)(f_2-f)$, which is our $\beta_2\Delta\omega_1\Delta\omega_2$, and the factor $16/27$ is the polarization-averaged Kerr coefficient for dual-polarization signals. The "phased-array" factor $\chi$ describes the interference of the contributions of $N_s$ spans; $\chi=1$ for a single span. (A caution on notation: Poggiolini uses $\alpha$ for the *field* loss coefficient and calls the PSDs $G$. We have converted to the power coefficient, $\alpha_{\mathrm{book}} = 2\alpha_{\mathrm{Pogg}}$, and reserved $G$ for amplifier gain.)

For rectangular channels of bandwidth $B_{\mathrm{ch}}$ on a grid of spacing $\Delta f$, $N_{\mathrm{ch}}$ channels, the double integral can be approximated in closed form at the centre channel {{S:poggiolini_jlt_2012}}:
$$
\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{NLI}}(0) \approx \frac{8}{27}\,\gamma^2\,\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{WDM}}^3\,L_{\mathrm{eff}}^2\;\frac{\mathrm{asinh}\!\left(\frac{\pi^2}{2}|\beta_2|L_{\mathrm{eff,a}}B_{\mathrm{ch}}^2N_{\mathrm{ch}}^{2B_{\mathrm{ch}}/\Delta f}\right)}{\pi|\beta_2|L_{\mathrm{eff,a}}} .
$$
The asinh grows only logarithmically with total bandwidth, which is the GN model's statement that FWM between widely separated frequencies is suppressed by phase mismatch. Poggiolini states the reliability envelope of the GN model as {{L:ch01.gn.validity_envelope_2012|v}}, and the closed form adds lower bounds on $|\beta_2|$, on span loss and on $B_{\mathrm{ch}}/\Delta f$; within them its NLI error at full C-band is below half a decibel.

### $P_{\mathrm{NLI}} = \eta P^3$

If the NLI is locally white over the channel (the "LWN" approximation) and the receiver noise bandwidth is $B_N = R_s$, then with $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{WDM}} = P_{\mathrm{ch}}/R_s$ for every channel,
$$
P_{\mathrm{NLI}} = \mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{NLI}}(0)\,R_s = \mu\left(\frac{P_{\mathrm{ch}}}{R_s}\right)^3R_s = \frac{\mu}{R_s^2}P_{\mathrm{ch}}^3 \equiv \eta\,P_{\mathrm{ch}}^3 ,
$$
where $\mu$ is everything in the closed form except $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{WDM}}^3$. The NLI efficiency $\eta$ is independent of power {{S:poggiolini_jlt_2014}}. For $N_s$ spans in the incoherent approximation, both the ASE and the NLI scale as $N_s$: $P_{\mathrm{ASE}} = N_sP_{\mathrm{ASE}}^{(1)}$ and $\eta = N_s\eta^{(1)}$. More generally Poggiolini writes $\eta = \eta^{(1)}N_s^{1+\varepsilon}$, where the coherent-accumulation exponent $\varepsilon$ is zero for incoherent accumulation and one for complete phase matching {{S:poggiolini_jlt_2012}}. The ASE in the same bandwidth $R_s$, counting both polarizations, is $P_{\mathrm{ASE}}^{(1)} = h\nu F(G-1)R_s$ per span {{S:poggiolini_jlt_2014}}.

The generalized SNR (广义信噪比, GSNR) is then {{S:poggiolini_jlt_2014}}
$$
\mathrm{GSNR} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{ch}}}{P_{\mathrm{ASE}} + \eta P_{\mathrm{ch}}^3}.
$$
It is the SNR seen by an ideal coherent receiver at the decision device, before transceiver impairments; it is *not* the OSNR of Section 1.5, which is referred to $B_{\mathrm{ref}}$.

### The optimum launch power and the 1/3 to 2/3 split

The GSNR rises linearly with $P_{\mathrm{ch}}$ at low power and falls as $P_{\mathrm{ch}}^{-2}$ at high power, so it has a maximum. Differentiate:
$$
\frac{d\,\mathrm{GSNR}}{dP_{\mathrm{ch}}} = \frac{(P_{\mathrm{ASE}}+\eta P_{\mathrm{ch}}^3) - P_{\mathrm{ch}}\cdot3\eta P_{\mathrm{ch}}^2}{(P_{\mathrm{ASE}}+\eta P_{\mathrm{ch}}^3)^2} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{ASE}} - 2\eta P_{\mathrm{ch}}^3}{(P_{\mathrm{ASE}}+\eta P_{\mathrm{ch}}^3)^2}.
$$
Setting the numerator to zero,
$$
P_{\mathrm{opt}} = \left(\frac{P_{\mathrm{ASE}}}{2\eta}\right)^{1/3},
$$
Poggiolini's Eq. (29) {{S:poggiolini_jlt_2014}}. At the optimum $\eta P_{\mathrm{opt}}^3 = P_{\mathrm{ASE}}/2$: the NLI is half the ASE, so of the total noise one third is NLI and two thirds ASE. Poggiolini states this for any $\varepsilon$ {{Q:ch01.gn.ase_eq_2nli}}. Substituting back,
$$
\mathrm{GSNR}_{\max} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{opt}}}{\tfrac32P_{\mathrm{ASE}}} = \frac23\,\frac{P_{\mathrm{opt}}}{P_{\mathrm{ASE}}} = \frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{4}{\eta}\right)^{1/3}P_{\mathrm{ASE}}^{-2/3}.
$$
(We derived this closed form ourselves from Poggiolini's Eqs. (28) and (29); it is not printed in the parts of the papers we read.) Three consequences follow, and they are the practical content of the GN model.

1. **Independence of distance.** With $\varepsilon=0$, $P_{\mathrm{ASE}}$ and $\eta$ both scale as $N_s$, so their ratio, and hence $P_{\mathrm{opt}}$, does not depend on the number of spans; Poggiolini notes that it is also independent of the target OSNR {{Q:ch01.gn.popt_indep_of_target_osnr}}. The GSNR at the optimum then falls exactly as $1/N_s$: $\mathrm{GSNR}_{\max}(N_s) = \mathrm{GSNR}_{\max}^{(1)}/N_s$, so the maximum reach for a required SNR is
$$
N_s^{\max} = \left\lfloor\frac{\mathrm{GSNR}_{\max}^{(1)}}{\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{req}}}\right\rfloor,
$$
equivalent to Poggiolini's Eq. (30) {{S:poggiolini_jlt_2012}}.
2. **The 1/3 dampening.** Since $\mathrm{GSNR}_{\max}\propto\eta^{-1/3}$, an error of $x$ dB in $\eta$ produces an error of only $x/3$ dB in the maximum GSNR, in $P_{\mathrm{opt}}$ and in the maximum reach; Poggiolini makes exactly this point for reach {{Q:ch01.gn.reach_vs_nli_one_third}}. The model can be crude and still be useful.
3. **The 2/3 lever on ASE.** Since $\mathrm{GSNR}_{\max}\propto P_{\mathrm{ASE}}^{-2/3}$, one decibel less noise figure or span loss buys two thirds of a decibel of maximum GSNR, not one decibel: part of the gain is spent on the lower optimum power. The same reasoning applied to the asinh form gives the fiber-design rule that a larger $|\beta_2|$ improves reach by a third of its decibel change and a larger effective area by two thirds {{Q:ch01.gn.reach_vs_dispersion_gamma}}, which is the quantitative case for G.654.E. Likewise, ideal nonlinearity compensation that halves the NLI buys only about one decibel of reach {{Q:ch01.gn.nlc_3db_gives_1db}}.

### Coherent accumulation, ε

For $\varepsilon>0$, $\eta$ grows faster than $N_s$, $P_{\mathrm{opt}}$ decreases slowly with distance, and the 1/3 to 2/3 split still holds. Poggiolini's best-fit values for full C-band systems are small: {{L:ch01.gn.eps_nyquist_5thz}} for Nyquist-WDM filling the C-band and {{L:ch01.gn.eps_rs_50ghz}} for the fixed-grid reference systems, with {{L:ch01.gn.eps_typical_2014}} given as the typical range in the 2014 tutorial. Single-channel, or narrow-bandwidth, systems have much larger $\varepsilon$ {{Q:ch01.gn.eps_rs_50ghz}} because their FWM products stay phase-matched over many spans.

### The EGN correction

The Gaussian-signal assumption is not exact: a QAM constellation has a smaller fourth moment than a Gaussian, so it generates less NLI, especially in the first spans, before dispersion has Gaussianized it. The EGN ("enhanced GN") model of Carena and coauthors adds a correction term, $\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{NLI}}^{\mathrm{EGN}} = \mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{NLI}}^{\mathrm{GN}} + \mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{NLI}}^{\mathrm{corr}}$ {{S:carena_oe_2014}}, whose self-channel part has the form $P^3[\kappa_1 + \Phi_a\kappa_2 + \Psi_a\kappa_3]$, with format-dependent coefficients built from the fourth and sixth moments of the constellation; for QPSK they are $(\Phi_a,\Psi_a)$ = {{L:ch01.egn.phi_qpsk|v}} {{S:carena_oe_2014}}. Because $\Phi_a<0$ for all QAM formats, the correction reduces the NLI. Format dependence (EGN) and coherent accumulation ($\varepsilon$) are distinct corrections.

How large is the correction? Carena reports that the GN model underestimates maximum reach by {{L:ch01.egn.gn_reach_underestimate}} for PM-QPSK and PM-16QAM over common fibers (up to {{L:ch01.egn.gn_reach_underestimate_ls}} over a very-low-dispersion fiber), that it overestimates the NLI power near maximum reach by {{L:ch01.egn.nli_overestimate_near_max_reach}}, and that EGN brings the reach error below {{L:ch01.egn.residual_error}}. The two statements are consistent through the 1/3 dampening: an NLI overestimate of one to two decibels maps to a reach underestimate of {{C:ex16_gn_optimum.egn_reach_gain_lo}} to {{C:ex16_gn_optimum.egn_reach_gain_hi}}, which our script checks against Carena's range. In other words, the GN model is conservative for QAM. In the first span the overestimate can be large, {{L:ch01.gn.first_span_overestimate_qpsk}} for PM-QPSK over SMF {{Q:ch01.gn.first_span_overestimate_qpsk}}, but the first span contributes little to the end-of-link NLI.

### Shannon capacity and the implementation gap

If NLI is Gaussian and white over the channel, the link is an additive white Gaussian noise channel with SNR equal to the GSNR, and the Shannon limit (香农极限) per polarization is
$$
C = \log_2(1+\mathrm{GSNR})\quad\text{bit per symbol per polarization},
$$
as used by Poggiolini for the optical capacity of GN-model links {{S:poggiolini_jlt_2014}}. This "capacity" is conditional on the GN model and on treating NLI as noise; it is not the capacity of the nonlinear fiber channel, which is unknown. Practical transceivers fall short of it for three reasons: square QAM is not a Gaussian input (the ultimate shaping gain, Chapter 2), finite-length FEC operates a margin above its threshold, and the transceiver itself adds noise and distortion. Yoshida cites transceiver SNRs of {{L:ch01.gap.transceiver_snr_range}} for high-baud-rate coherent transceivers {{Q:ch01.gap.transceiver_snr_range}}. Because transceiver noise and line noise are independent, they add in inverse SNR: $\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{-1} = \mathrm{GSNR}^{-1} + \mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{TR}}^{-1}$, so a transceiver whose own SNR is only a few decibels above the format threshold consumes most of the line's budget. In GN-based capacity studies, Poggiolini assumed a {{L:ch01.cap.oct_assumed_margin}} margin plus {{L:ch01.cap.soft_fec_gap}} soft-FEC penalty from the ideal limit, which are assumptions rather than measurements.

### Accuracy in the field

The open-source GNPy tool implements the GN model. Against 500 measured samples over mixed-fiber, Raman-amplified, multivendor links from several hundred to several thousand kilometres, its GSNR predictions were within {{L:ch01.gnpy.gsnr_accuracy}} for the large majority of samples and its optimum launch power within {{L:ch01.gnpy.popt_accuracy}} {{Q:ch01.gnpy.gsnr_accuracy}}.

## Frontier fibers and open questions

The preceding sections treated the constants of silica as fixed: a Rayleigh floor near the record values of Section 1.1, a group index near {{L:ch01.fiber.smf28ultra_group_index_1550|v}}, a nonlinear index of a few $10^{-20}\,\mathrm{m^2/W}$, and one spatial mode. The two frontier technologies of this section attack these constants directly. Hollow-core fiber (空芯光纤) moves the light out of the glass; space-division multiplexing (空分复用, SDM) puts more than one spatial channel into one fiber.

### Antiresonant guidance

A hollow core has a lower index than any glass cladding, so total internal reflection cannot guide light in it. Antiresonant hollow-core fiber (反谐振空芯光纤) guides by a different mechanism. The core is surrounded by thin glass membranes, here the walls of small capillary tubes, of thickness $t$ and index $n$. Treat a membrane as a Fabry–Pérot etalon at near-grazing incidence. The transverse wavenumber in the glass is $k_0\sqrt{n^2-n_{\mathrm{eff}}^2}\approx k_0\sqrt{n^2-1}$ because the core mode has $n_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx1$. The membrane is *resonant*, and transparent to light leaking outward, when the round-trip transverse phase is a multiple of $2\pi$:
$$
2k_0t\sqrt{n^2-1} = 2\pi m \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda_m = \frac{2t\sqrt{n^2-1}}{m}, \qquad m = 1,2,\dots
$$
Between resonances the membrane reflects strongly back toward the core. The nested antiresonant nodeless fiber (嵌套反谐振无节点光纤, NANF), proposed by Poletti, adds a second, smaller tube inside each cladding tube, which adds a second antiresonant reflection, and keeps the tubes from touching (nodeless), which removes the extra resonances that contact points create. His simulations showed that total loss from leakage, surface scattering and bending could in principle fall below that of solid fibers, and that resonant out-coupling of higher-order modes makes the fiber effectively single-mode {{Q:ch01.hcf.poletti2014_nanf_proposal}}. The double nested antiresonant nodeless fiber (双层嵌套反谐振无节点光纤, DNANF) nests two tubes inside each outer tube.

Why is the loss floor of such a fiber not set by Rayleigh scattering in glass? Because only a tiny fraction of the modal power overlaps the glass membranes, and the Rayleigh coefficient of the air or gas in the core is negligible compared with that of glass. The loss is instead the sum of confinement (leakage) loss, which falls steeply with more and better antiresonant layers, scattering from frozen-in capillary waves on the membrane surfaces, microbending, and gas absorption lines. None of these has the fundamental $\lambda^{-4}$ floor of silica, which is why the hollow-core records below could fall under the silica record.

### Latency

The same small glass overlap gives a group index close to one. Group delay per unit length is $n_g/c$, so the latency is {{C:ex17_hcf_latency.t_smf}} in SMF-28 Ultra (with $n_g$ = {{L:ch01.fiber.smf28ultra_group_index_1550}}) and {{C:ex17_hcf_latency.t_hcf}} in hollow core, taking $n_g$ = 1 as measured approximately by Antesberger and coauthors in a preprint {{Q:ch01.hcf.ng_nanf_vs_smf28}}. The difference is {{C:ex17_hcf_latency.dt}}, a latency reduction of $1-n_{g,\mathrm{HCF}}/n_{g,\mathrm{SMF}}$ = {{C:ex17_hcf_latency.lat_red_pct}} and a speed increase of $n_{g,\mathrm{SMF}}/n_{g,\mathrm{HCF}}-1$ = {{C:ex17_hcf_latency.speedup_pct}}. Poletti's 2013 photonic-bandgap result, light at {{C:ex17_hcf_latency.poletti_frac}} of the vacuum speed, implies a saving of {{C:ex17_hcf_latency.dt_poletti_check}}, in agreement with the {{L:ch01.hcf.poletti2013_speed_997pct}} reported. Published round numbers vary: Petrovich and coauthors write a {{L:ch01.hcf.petrovich2025_speed_45pct}} speed increase in their abstract and a {{L:ch01.hcf.petrovich2025_latency_30pct}} latency reduction in their conclusion; Poggiolini and Poletti write {{L:ch01.hcf.poggiolini2022_speed_50pct}} faster; YOFC writes {{L:ch01.hcf.yofc_ar2025_latency_31pct}} lower latency; Antesberger measured {{L:ch01.hcf.latency_reduction_antesberger}} over slightly unequal fiber lengths (preprint). The two physical quantities, latency reduction and speed increase, are different functions of the same ratio, and the quoted figures are roundings of the computed pair.

For the transmission physics of the earlier sections the consequences are equally large. The nonlinear coefficient falls drastically because the core is gas, so the GN model's NLI term nearly vanishes and the launch power can rise until amplifiers limit it. The chromatic dispersion is small; Petrovich and coauthors report about six times lower than standard fiber {{Q:ch01.hcf.petrovich2025_latency_30pct}}.

### Space-division multiplexing

SDM fibers multiply the number of spatial channels. Puttnam and coauthors classify them as uncoupled multicore fiber (多芯光纤), multimode and few-mode fiber (少模光纤), few-mode multicore fiber, and coupled-core multicore fiber {{Q:ch01.sdm.puttnam2021_classification}}. The classes differ in one quantity that determines the receiver: the strength of linear coupling between spatial channels relative to the link length.

*Weakly coupled multicore fiber* (弱耦合多芯光纤) keeps crosstalk between cores low enough that each core is an independent single-mode fiber with its own standard transceiver; there is no MIMO cost, only crosstalk and the engineering of fan-in/fan-out devices. *Coupled-core* and *few-mode* fibers deliberately allow strong coupling. Then the $M$ spatial modes, each with two polarizations, form a $2M\times2M$ linear channel, the natural generalization of the $2\times2$ Jones matrix of Section 1.3. Exactly as for PMD, a unitary channel can be inverted, but the equalizer must now be $2M\times2M$, and its memory must span the *modal* group-delay spread. If the coupling is strong and random, the same random-walk argument as in Section 1.3 gives a delay spread growing as $\sqrt L$ rather than $L$. The equalizer complexity per symbol therefore scales roughly as $(2M)^2$ times the number of taps, and the number of taps scales with $R_s$ times the delay spread; per spatial channel, the cost grows as $M$ times the tap count. Long-haul transmission records in randomly coupled multicore fiber with full MIMO DSP have been reported at conferences; we discuss the figures, which our sources support only at abstract level, in the contested box.

Standardization favours the simpler option. ITU-T G.Sup87 prioritizes weakly coupled MCF with standard cladding and backward compatibility with G.65x fibers as the first SDM fiber to standardize {{Q:ch01.sdm.gsup87_wcmcf_first_priority}} and expects at least {{L:ch01.sdm.gsup87_timeline}} to establish the first Recommendation. Study Group 15 agreed in October 2025 to establish two Recommendations for weakly coupled MCF, one compatible with G.652/G.657 and one with G.654 {{Q:ch01.sdm.itu_sg15_2025_two_mcf_recs}}, with a target completion of {{L:ch01.sdm.gsmmcf_target_2028}} {{Q:ch01.sdm.gsmmcf_target_2028}}.

## Problems

**Problem 1 (Rayleigh scaling).** ITU-T G.652 extends the O-band attenuation specification to shorter wavelengths by adding a fixed Rayleigh increment {{L:ch01.att.g652d_rayleigh_extension_1260}} between the two ends of the extension. (a) Using $\alpha_R = A_R/\lambda^4$, derive the $A_R$ that this increment implies. (b) Compare it with the fiber range {{L:ch01.phys.rayleigh_coeff_fiber}} and explain why a specification might choose a larger value.

**Problem 2 (dispersion curve).** Starting from $D(\lambda) = \tfrac{S_0\lambda}{4}[1-(\lambda_0/\lambda)^4]$, derive $S(\lambda)$ and show that $S(\lambda_0) = S_0$. Evaluate $D$ and $S$ at the C-band centre for $\lambda_0$ at the middle of the G.652.D range and $S_0$ at its maximum.

**Problem 3 (why coherent receivers need CD compensation).** Show that the group-delay spread across a signal of bandwidth $R_s$ after a length $L$ is $\Delta\tau \approx |D|L\lambda^2R_s/c$. Evaluate it, and the number of symbols it spans, for a {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.rs_gbd}} signal after a thousand kilometres of fiber with the typical G.652 dispersion.

**Problem 4 (O-band fading).** The 400G-LR4-10 specification allows channel dispersion as negative as the lower value of {{L:ch01.cd.lr4_10_channel_cd}}. Assuming this occurs at the short edge of the grid, find the first power-fading null and compare it with the Nyquist frequency of a {{C:ex12_power_fading.rs_gbd}} lane.

**Problem 5 (PMD and the coherent equalizer).** For a thousand-kilometre link at the G.652.D PMD limit, compute the mean DGD and the DGD exceeded with probability {{C:ex13_pmd_outage.p_ratio3}}. Express the latter in symbol periods at {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.rs_gbd}}. What does this imply for the length of the $2\times2$ MIMO equalizer?

**Problem 6 (effective length).** For the span of the GN reference system (Section 1.6), compute $L_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and the fraction $L_{\mathrm{eff}}/L_{\mathrm{eff,a}}$. Show that $L_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is within one percent of its asymptote once the span loss exceeds $10\log_{10}100$, and find the corresponding length.

**Problem 7 (inversion factor).** A low-noise test amplifier has the noise figure {{L:ch01.amp.viavi_lownoise_nf}} at a gain of at least the value in its datasheet record {{Q:ch01.amp.viavi_lownoise_nf}}. Find $n_{\mathrm{sp}}$ and the excess over the quantum limit.

**Problem 8 (the O-band constant).** Repeat the derivation of the fifty-eight-decibel constant at {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam1310}} with the same {{L:ch01.osnr.gsup39_bref_typical}} reference bandwidth. Show that the constant shifts by $30\log_{10}(\lambda_1/\lambda_2)$ between two wavelengths.

**Problem 9 (required OSNR).** Using the required GSNR of the Section 1.6 worked example (best-transceiver case), find the OSNR in {{L:ch01.osnr.gsup39_bref_typical}} that a {{C:ex15_osnr_spans.rs_gbd}} dual-polarization receiver needs.

**Problem 10 (noise-figure lever).** Using $\mathrm{GSNR}_{\max}\propto\eta^{-1/3}P_{\mathrm{ASE}}^{-2/3}$, show that reducing the amplifier noise figure by one decibel increases the maximum reach by a factor $10^{2/30}$. Apply it to the Section 1.6 example.

**Problem 11 (latency).** Compute the one-way latency saving of a thousand kilometres of hollow-core fiber over SMF-28 Ultra, and explain why the "latency reduction" and "speed increase" quoted for hollow-core fiber are different numbers.

**Problem 12 (Shannon at threshold).** Evaluate the Shannon limit per polarization at the theoretical 16-QAM threshold SNR of Section 1.6 and compare it with the four coded bits per polarization that 16-QAM carries. Interpret the difference.

## Solutions

**Solution 1.** (a) $\Delta\alpha = A_R(\lambda_1^{-4}-\lambda_2^{-4})$ with $\lambda$ in µm; the bracket is {{C:ex11_span_budget.ext_1260_factor}}, so $A_R$ = {{C:problems.p1_A}}. (b) This is {{C:problems.p1_ratio}} times the upper end of the measured fiber range. The specification must cover fibers with additional short-wavelength loss (for example, small-angle scattering and the UV tail in heavily doped cores) and is written as a bound, so a conservative increment is appropriate.

**Solution 2.** $dD/d\lambda = \tfrac{S_0}{4}[1-(\lambda_0/\lambda)^4] + \tfrac{S_0\lambda}{4}\cdot4\lambda_0^4\lambda^{-5} = \tfrac{S_0}{4}[1+3(\lambda_0/\lambda)^4]$, which equals $S_0$ at $\lambda=\lambda_0$. With $\lambda_0$ = {{C:problems.p2_l0}} and $S_0$ at its maximum, $D$ = {{C:problems.p2_D}} and $S$ = {{C:problems.p2_S}}. The formula, intended for the O-band, lands within the C-band specification range {{L:ch01.cd.g652d_d1550_range}}.

**Solution 3.** The delay difference between frequencies separated by $\Delta\omega$ is $\beta_2L\Delta\omega$; with $\Delta\omega = 2\pi R_s$ and $\beta_2 = -D\lambda^2/2\pi c$ this gives $|D|L\lambda^2R_s/c$. Numerically $\Delta\tau$ = {{C:problems.p3_dtau_ns}}, or {{C:problems.p3_symbols}}. Coherent DSP therefore compensates bulk dispersion in the frequency domain (Chapter 2).

**Solution 4.** $f_1 = \sqrt{c/(2\lambda^2|DL|)}$ with $|DL|$ in s/m. The result is {{C:problems.p4_fnull}}, above the Nyquist frequency {{C:ex12_power_fading.nyq_ghz}} by a factor of about one and a half.

**Solution 5.** $\langle\Delta\tau\rangle = D_{\mathrm{PMD}}\sqrt L$ = {{C:problems.p5_mean}}. The Maxwellian tail at three times the mean gives the stated probability, so the DGD to tolerate is {{C:problems.p5_dgd3}}, or {{C:problems.p5_symbols}}. The equalizer's impulse response must span this plus the residual chromatic dispersion and filter memory; a handful of taps suffices for PMD, which is why PMD is no longer a design constraint for coherent links.

**Solution 6.** With the reference-system loss, $L_{\mathrm{eff}}$ = {{C:problems.p6_leff}} and $L_{\mathrm{eff}}/L_{\mathrm{eff,a}} = 1-e^{-\alpha L}$ = {{C:problems.p6_frac}}. $L_{\mathrm{eff}}\geq0.99L_{\mathrm{eff,a}}$ requires $e^{-\alpha L}\leq0.01$, that is $\alpha L\geq\ln100$, or a span loss of at least $10\log_{10}100$, which is twenty decibels; for this fiber the length is {{C:problems.p6_L99}}.

**Solution 7.** Invert $F = 2n_{\mathrm{sp}}(G-1)/G + 1/G$: $n_{\mathrm{sp}} = (FG-1)/(2(G-1))$ = {{C:problems.p7_nsp}}. The excess over $10\log_{10}2$ is {{C:problems.p7_excess}}.

**Solution 8.** $h\nu B_{\mathrm{ref}} = h(c/\lambda)(c\Delta\lambda/\lambda^2) = hc^2\Delta\lambda/\lambda^3$, so the constant $-10\log_{10}(h\nu B_{\mathrm{ref}})$ changes by $30\log_{10}(\lambda_1/\lambda_2)$ between two wavelengths at fixed $\Delta\lambda$. At {{C:ex12_power_fading.lam1310}} it is {{C:problems.p8_k1310}}, lower by {{C:problems.p8_diff}}: an O-band photon carries more energy and a fixed wavelength interval spans more hertz.

**Solution 9.** $\mathrm{OSNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} = \mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}} + 10\log_{10}(R_s/B_{\mathrm{ref}})$ for $p=2$, giving {{C:problems.p9_osnr_req}}.

**Solution 10.** $\mathrm{GSNR}_{\max}\propto P_{\mathrm{ASE}}^{-2/3}$ and $P_{\mathrm{ASE}}\propto F$, so one decibel less NF gives {{C:problems.p10_gain_db}} more GSNR. Since $N_s^{\max}\propto\mathrm{GSNR}_{\max}^{(1)}$ (for $\varepsilon=0$), the reach grows by $10^{(2/3)/10}$ = {{C:problems.p10_reach_factor}}. In the worked example this raises the maximum span count from {{C:ex16_gn_optimum.nmax}} to {{C:problems.p10_nmax}}.

**Solution 11.** $\Delta t = (n_{g,\mathrm{SMF}}-n_{g,\mathrm{HCF}})L/c$ = {{C:problems.p11_dt_ms}}. The latency reduction is $1-n_{g,\mathrm{HCF}}/n_{g,\mathrm{SMF}}$ = {{C:ex17_hcf_latency.lat_red_pct}}, while the speed increase is $n_{g,\mathrm{SMF}}/n_{g,\mathrm{HCF}}-1$ = {{C:ex17_hcf_latency.speedup_pct}}; one is the relative change in time, the other in speed.

**Solution 12.** $\log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR})$ at {{C:ex16_gn_optimum.snr_theory_db}} is {{C:problems.p12_cpol}}, slightly above the four *raw* bits per polarization and well above the net rate of {{C:ex16_gn_optimum.net_bits}} for both polarizations (half of it per polarization) after {{C:ex16_gn_optimum.fec_oh_pct}} FEC overhead. The FEC operates far from capacity because the pre-FEC threshold BER is reached at an SNR where a capacity-achieving code could carry more information per symbol; shaping and stronger codes close this gap (Chapter 2).
